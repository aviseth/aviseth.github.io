104 of 152 intervals contain zero. Two thirds of the list cannot be told apart from an average Test batsman. Everything below is written in the shadow of that.

01 · The situation

Forty is not forty.

A batting average divides runs by dismissals and treats every dismissal as the same event. Forty against Steyn and Philander at 20 for 3 counts exactly what forty on a flat deck at 300 for 2 counts. Everyone watching knows those are different innings. The average does not, because a scorecard cannot see the difference — it records that a man batted at four, not whether four meant the eighth over or after tea.

Ball-by-ball data can. Walking each innings delivery by delivery recovers the score on the board at the moment each batsman walked out. Every dot below is one innings of one career.

big score middling cheap — horizontal axis: team score when he came in · vertical: wickets already down

The four situations are cut by runs per wicket on the board at entry: opening, collapse (under 20), rebuild, and platform (35 and above). Across the whole dataset they split 6,633 / 7,712 / 7,995 / 10,364 innings, so none of them is a curiosity. The gap between a man’s collapse average and his platform average is one of the more revealing numbers here, and it tracks reputation far less neatly than you would expect.

02 · The argument

The weights are the question, not the answer.

Eleven measures go into the composite: average, versatility by region and against pace and spin, away record, scoring under pressure, record against strong attacks, impact, an era index, volume, hard pitches, fourth innings. Each is scored 0–100 within the cohort, then blended.

And the blend is invented. Defensible, argued over, tuned — but there is no fact about whether a big away hundred is worth more than ten years of turning up. That is a question about what you value in a cricketer, not a question about cricket. So rather than hide it behind a number, here it is. Move anything.

He loses it only when volume is deliberately discounted, and then he loses it to Brook, whose short career is precisely what discounting volume flatters. Cook climbs when accumulation is rewarded; Trott climbs when adversity is. None of that is a fault in the ranking. It is the ranking telling you the answer depends on the question.

03 · Above expectation

Runs the situation did not ask for.

The measure I trust most ignores the composite entirely. A Poisson model predicts how many runs an average Test batsman would make from a given position, in a given match situation, against a given quality of attack, on a given pitch. A man’s impact is what he actually made, minus that. It is fitted only on innings that ended in a dismissal — a not-out is censored data, the batsman was not finished — and reported per innings, so longevity cannot win on its own.

Then the error bars went on, by resampling each man’s own innings, and rather deflated the exercise.

Bars are 95% bootstrap intervals. Where two overlap, the order between them is not meaningful.

Those are the batsmen whose contribution above an average Test player cannot be distinguished from nothing at all on the evidence available.

Two thirds of the list. Not because those men are bad — the pool already demands 45 Test innings, a career most cricketers never get — but because a batting career is a small sample of a noisy process, and telling two good batsmen apart takes more innings than most careers contain. Brook’s interval runs from +4.9 to +30.4. Root’s, on 304 innings, runs from +9.1 to +19.3. Brook’s point estimate is higher; Root’s is far better established. Anyone confident about which is better is telling you about themselves.

Two corrections were needed before that chart meant anything. The first run had Daniel Vettori ninth, on a career average of 35.9 — not a bug, but a consequence of adjusting expectation for batting position: a number eight who bats well clears a number eight’s bar far more easily than a number four clears his. The pool is now specialist batsmen only. The second was subtler: opposition Elo and attack quality were both in the model, correlated at r = 0.66, and Elo’s coefficient came out negative — implying a stronger opponent made scoring easier. Attack quality is what a batsman actually faces; Elo also contains the opponent’s batting, which has no bearing on it. Dropping Elo fixed the sign and cost nothing.
