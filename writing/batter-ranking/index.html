Nobody agrees what a great batsman is.

So I stopped trying to settle it. This is a ranking of 89 modern Test batsmen where you set the weights, the situation each man walked into is counted, and the error bars are printed — which is how you find out that most of the order is noise.

Who is in this

Modern means debuted 2008 or later.

Four rules, all of them arguable, none of them hidden:

  • Debuted in 2008 or later. Taken from the batsman’s real career span, not from the first innings this dataset happens to contain. That distinction matters: de Villiers played 2004–2018, so the data sees all of him, but he belongs to the generation before this one and is out. So are Amla, Clarke, Bell, Pietersen, Dhoni and Hussey.
  • At least 45 Test innings (80 for the stricter cohort).
  • At least 90% of his career visible. Ball‑by‑ball records start in 2001 and carry only 23 Tests before 2003, so “first innings we can see” is not a debut — Sangakkara first appears in 2003 having started in 2000. Checking observed innings against the real career total is the only honest test, and it keeps out Tendulkar (42% visible), Gilchrist (42%) and Hayden (47%), whose numbers here would be a fragment presented as a career.
  • A specialist batsman — median batting position seven or higher. The previous version of this had James Anderson, Stuart Broad and Nathan Lyon in a ranking of batting.

That leaves 89 batsmen, and the same 89 in both the composite and the impact model — those two used to disagree on who was even eligible.

Now the error bars

Ask instead how many runs each man made above what his situation asked for, and resample his own innings to see how firm the answer is.

70 of the 89 intervals contain zero. Most of the list cannot be told apart from an average Test batsman. Everything below is written in the shadow of that.

01 · The situation

Forty is not forty.

A batting average divides runs by dismissals and treats every dismissal as the same event. Forty against Steyn and Philander at 20 for 3 counts exactly what forty on a flat deck at 300 for 2 counts. Everyone watching knows those are different innings. The average does not, because a scorecard cannot see the difference — it records that a man batted at four, not whether four meant the eighth over or after tea.

Ball-by-ball data can. Walking each innings delivery by delivery recovers the score on the board at the moment each batsman walked out. Every dot below is one innings of one career.

big score middling cheap — horizontal axis: team score when he came in · vertical: wickets already down

The four situations are cut by runs per wicket on the board at entry: opening, collapse (under 20), rebuild, and platform (35 and above). Across the whole dataset they split 6,633 / 7,712 / 7,995 / 10,364 innings, so none of them is a curiosity. The gap between a man’s collapse average and his platform average is one of the more revealing numbers here, and it tracks reputation far less neatly than you would expect.

02 · The argument

The weights are the question, not the answer.

Eleven measures go into the composite: average, versatility by region and against pace and spin, away record, scoring under pressure, record against strong attacks, impact, an era index, volume, hard pitches, fourth innings. Each is scored 0–100 within the cohort, then blended.

And the blend is invented. Defensible, argued over, tuned — but there is no fact about whether a big away hundred is worth more than ten years of turning up. That is a question about what you value in a cricketer, not a question about cricket. So rather than hide it behind a number, here it is. Move anything.

Smith holds the top under most weightings; Root and Brook trade second and third. Below them it stops being a ranking and starts being a scrum — Kohli, Jaiswal, Head, Trott and Pant sit within five points of one another on the default blend, comfortably inside what their innings counts can actually support. Move one slider and that order rearranges. None of this is a bug; it is the ranking telling you the answer depends on the question.

Kohli at fifth is the case worth staring at. He gets there on volume, his record against the strongest attacks and the era adjustment — not on the things the eye remembers. His average in his worst region is 28.4 and his average walking into a collapse is 32.6, both near the bottom of this cohort. Whether that makes him fifth or twelfth depends entirely on what you chose to reward.

03 · Above expectation

Runs the situation did not ask for.

The measure I trust most ignores the composite entirely. A Poisson model predicts how many runs an average Test batsman would make from a given position, in a given match situation, against a given quality of attack, on a given pitch. A man’s impact is what he actually made, minus that. It is fitted only on innings that ended in a dismissal — a not-out is censored data, the batsman was not finished — and reported per innings, so longevity cannot win on its own.

Then the error bars went on, by resampling each man’s own innings, and rather deflated the exercise.

Bars are 95% bootstrap intervals. Where two overlap, the order between them is not meaningful.

Those are the batsmen whose contribution above an average Test player cannot be distinguished from nothing at all on the evidence available.

Seventy-nine per cent of the list. Not because those men are bad — the pool already demands 45 Test innings, a career most cricketers never get — but because a batting career is a small sample of a noisy process, and telling two good batsmen apart takes more innings than most careers contain. Brook’s interval runs from +4.9 to +30.4. Root’s, on 304 innings, runs from +9.1 to +19.3. Brook’s point estimate is higher; Root’s is far better established. Anyone confident about which is better is telling you about themselves.

Two corrections were needed before that chart meant anything. The first run had Daniel Vettori ninth, on a career average of 35.9 — not a bug, but a consequence of adjusting expectation for batting position: a number eight who bats well clears a number eight’s bar far more easily than a number four clears his. The pool is now specialist batsmen only. The second was subtler: opposition Elo and attack quality were both in the model, correlated at r = 0.66, and Elo’s coefficient came out negative — implying a stronger opponent made scoring easier. Attack quality is what a batsman actually faces; Elo also contains the opponent’s batting, which has no bearing on it. Dropping Elo fixed the sign and cost nothing.

06 · Crisis and comfort

Who actually holds up.

Horizontal: average walking onto a platform, when someone else has already done the hard part. Vertical: average walking into a collapse. The diagonal is indifference to circumstance. Above it means better when the roof is falling in — and there are far fewer of those than reputation suggests.

07 · The two games

Pace on one axis, spin on the other.

Test batting is really two skills that happen to share a scorecard. The bowler classification here is inferred from the deliveries themselves — stumpings conceded, share of new-ball overs, boundary rate — because the registry that used to supply it is gone. It agrees with 37 of 38 bowlers I checked by hand.

08 · Convergence

How slowly an average settles.

Cumulative average, innings by innings. Every line starts as noise — a hundred in the second Test puts a man on 70 — and takes a hundred innings to stop lurching. It is the same point the error bars make, drawn from the other direction: careers are short relative to the variance in them.
