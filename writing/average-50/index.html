He only averages thirty‑eight.

Said as an insult. It is, in fact, a number that puts a man in the top quarter of everyone who has ever had a Test career. Here is the whole distribution — every batter, every average — so you can see what the words actually mean.

The population

Three thousand two hundred and fourteen men have walked out to bat in a Test match. Most of them barely batted at all — a nightwatchman here, a fast bowler there, a one‑cap wonder from 1889.

So start with the ones who had a career: 1,206 players with at least twenty Test innings. Every dot below is one of them.

Sort them

Now line them up by career batting average — runs scored, divided by times dismissed. Low on the left, high on the right.

They stack into a shape. That shape is the thing everybody argues about without ever having looked at it.

The shape

Not one hill but two. There is a bowlers’ hump around 13 — the men who bat because someone has to — and a batters’ hump around 27. Then a long, thinning slope to the right.

The mean is 26.9, the median 26.4. That is the centre of gravity of Test batting: a man who averages twenty‑six is, precisely, average.

Thirty

Thirty sounds like nothing. A number you’d apologise for.

Fewer than half of all Test batters get there. 505 of 1,206 — 41.9%. Averaging thirty in Test cricket already puts you in the upper half of everyone who has ever done the job.

Thirty‑five

Now it starts to bite. 344 players. 28.5%.

Barely a quarter of Test batters have ever averaged thirty‑five over a career. The bar you thought was mediocre is the top third of the sport.

Forty

Forty is the number people actually mean when they say “a proper Test batter”.

214 players. 17.7%. Fewer than one in five. In 148 years, across twelve nations and something like two thousand Test matches, two hundred and fourteen men have averaged forty.

Forty‑five

109 players. 9.0%.

One in eleven. This is roughly the point at which a batter stops being good and starts being someone you build a side around.

Fifty

44 players. 3.6%.

Forty‑four men, out of 1,206, out of 3,214 who ever batted. That is the entire list — Tendulkar and Sobers and Sangakkara and Root and forty others. Averaging fifty in Test cricket is a one‑in‑twenty‑seven event.

And then

The distribution has a standard deviation of about 13. Bradman sits 5.6 standard deviations above the mean.

To match him in another sport you would need a sprinter running the 100m in roughly seven and a half seconds. He is not the top of this chart. He is off it.

Who counts?

Move the bar for what a career is.

Twenty innings is a choice, not a law. Drag it. At one innings you are including every tailender who ever nicked off; at a hundred you have only the marathon careers. The more you demand of a career, the better the survivors look — and even among the 219 men who batted a hundred times, only one in eight averages fifty.

Era

Was it ever easier?

Yes — briefly. Between the wars, on covered-in batting paradises with two-man attacks, ten percent of Test batters averaged fifty. It has never happened again. Every curve below holds the same number of players it had; they are drawn as densities so eras of different size can be compared.

Notes

Data. Career batting records for every man to have played a Test match, 1877 to 2025, sourced from the cricket_batting dataset in Rob J Hyndman’s weird R package, which compiles ESPNcricinfo career records. 3,241 players; 27 never batted; 70 more were never dismissed, so have no defined average. That leaves 3,144 batters with a career average, of whom 1,206 have twenty or more innings.

Averages. Runs ÷ (innings − not outs), computed from the raw counts rather than taken on trust. Players who appeared for more than one country are counted once, filed under “Multiple”. Seventeen pairs of players share a name and initials; those are disambiguated by debut year.

Eras. A player is assigned to the era containing the midpoint of his career, so a man who played 1990–2004 lands in 2000–2014. Densities are Gaussian kernel estimates using Silverman’s rule; they are smoothing, not truth — the dots underneath are the data.

The obvious caveat. A batting average takes no view on when the runs came, against whom, or on what. It does not know about uncovered pitches, or Bodyline, or the West Indies attack of 1984, or a flat deck in Colombo. It is a blunt instrument. The point of this page is not that the average is a perfect measure — it is that people quote it constantly while having no idea what its distribution looks like.
